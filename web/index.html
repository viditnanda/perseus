What Perseus 6 does, and what is new in 6.1

Perseus 6 is a complete rewrite, ten years after Perseus 4. It offers:

  • Rational barcodes with a proof. One run gives you the barcode over Q and all bad primes: the primes p for which the Fp barcode is different. For each bad prime it tells you exactly which intervals change. The rational computation is exact, so the certificate is a proof, and on typical data it costs little more than computing over a single prime field.
  • General inputs. Vietoris–Rips filtrations of point clouds and distance matrices, clique filtrations of weighted graphs, cubical complexes (images and volumes, including periodic ones, straight from numpy), simplicial complexes, simplicial sets, and filtered chain complexes over Z.
  • Integral answers. Homology over Z of filtered complexes, images of H*(Ks; Z) → H*(Kt; Z), and the torsion of H*(Kt; Z) at every step of a filtration; magnitude homology of graphs; chain complexes over Z[t, t−1]; homology and cohomology rings of finite groups.
  • Speed. On 2D images it is faster than GUDHI's dedicated image algorithm; on volumes and 4D grids it is 2–3× faster than Cubical Ripser and 5–15× faster than GUDHI; for Vietoris–Rips in degree 2 and up it is usually much faster than Ripser and than edge collapse followed by Ripser; for group homology it is often hundreds of times faster than HAP. See performance, including where other tools win.
For example, take 1024 points sampled from O(3), which is two copies of SO(3) ≅ RP3. Perseus proves that 2 is the only bad prime:
  • over Q, two H1 intervals, one in each copy, die at 1.288 and 1.307;
  • over F2, those two intervals live forever, and two H2 intervals are born at exactly those times.
This is the Z/2 in H1(RP3; Z), detected and located in the filtration.

New in 6.1

  • The default certificate is always a proof. In 6.0 the default fell back to random primes when 64-bit integers overflowed; now exact big rationals take over where needed, and they are fast enough that this costs little. Random primes run only on request (--mode fast), clearly labelled as not a proof.
  • New engines for cubical complexes: a planar engine for 2D images (6.5–26× faster than 6.0 in our tests) and a discrete-gradient engine for 3D and 4D grids (2–4× faster than 6.0, with less memory).
  • New inputs and integral outputs: torsion along filtrations (--torsion), integral homology and images (--integral, --image), simplicial sets, nerves and bar constructions, magnitude homology, Laurent coefficients, and finite groups.
  • Getting-started examples for every input, as shell scripts and Python scripts with their data (below).
  • Disconnected Vietoris–Rips filtrations are split into their components, reduced in parallel, also for the certified rational barcode: 8,192 points of O(3) through H3 take 2.2 s instead of 6.4 s, in half the memory.
  • Writing output files is about 8× faster, with byte-for-byte the same files.

The full list is in the changelog. Everything 6.0 computed, 6.1 computes identically, except that --mode auto now proves its answer.

Download, install, compile

The current version is 6.1.0. Download the executable for your operating system, install the Python module, or compile from source.

Linuxx86-64 · static macOSApple silicon macOSIntel Windows64-bit Source codeGitHub · MIT Pythonpip install perseus-tda

The executables are command-line programs: run them from a terminal. On macOS you may need to allow the downloaded file under System Settings → Privacy & Security, and make it executable with chmod +x. perseus --version prints the version. To compile from source, you need a C++17 compiler with 128-bit integers: g++ 9+ or clang 10+ (on Windows, MinGW-w64; Microsoft's compiler lacks 128-bit integers).

make                                              # builds ./perseus
make test                                         # optional: differential tests
make examples                                     # optional: runs every example
cd python && pip install --no-build-isolation .   # optional: Python module

The Python module needs pybind11 and numpy; the tests use Ripser as an oracle if it is installed.

Bug reports and feature requests are welcome on the issue tracker, or by email with “Perseus” in the subject line.

Basic usage

perseus <format> <input file> [<output prefix>] [options]

Some examples:

perseus points cloud.txt --maxdim 2             # Rips over Q, with the bad primes
perseus points cloud.txt --maxdim 2 --coeff 3   # only the barcode over F_3
perseus distance dist.csv --maxdim 1 --fp 2,3   # over Q, plus F_2 and F_3
perseus npy image.npy --periodic 0              # an image, periodic in axis 0
perseus simplices complex.txt out               # writes out_k.txt, out_betti.txt, out_cert.json
perseus simplices complex.txt --torsion         # and the torsion of H_q(K_t; Z) at every t
perseus cells complex.txt --integral --image 0,1   # H_*(K; Z) and the image of H_*(K_0; Z) -> H_*(K_1; Z)

Coefficients, bad primes and certificates

Over Z, persistence modules do not decompose into intervals, so there is no integral barcode. Perseus reports the next best thing:

  • the barcode over Q, and
  • the set of bad primes: those p for which the barcode over Fp (same filtration, degrees 0 to maxdim) differs from the one over Q. For each bad prime you get the intervals that appear only over Fp and those that appear only over Q.

Precisely, p is bad in degree q exactly when some relative homology group Hq(Kj, Ki; Z) or Hq−1(Ki; Z) of the filtration has p-torsion (a refinement of a theorem of Obayashi and Yoshiwaki, proved in docs/CERTIFICATE.md). So the bad primes record where the filtration carries integral torsion, and every other prime gives the rational answer. For filtrations of planar complexes, such as 2D images without periodic axes, there is never any torsion, so there are no bad primes; Perseus proves this without arithmetic, since the boundary matrices involved are totally unimodular.

How the certificate works

  1. The rational reduction. A prime that divides no pivot value of the reduction over Q cannot change the barcode. These pivot values are invariants of the filtered complex: ratios of minors of the boundary matrix.
  2. Exact arithmetic. By default the reduction over Q is exact: 64-bit numerators and denominators with every operation checked for overflow, and big rationals in a degree where they overflow, as happens with heavy torsion. The barcode and the pivot values are exact, so the certificate is a proof. With --mode fast, Perseus reduces modulo a few primes drawn at random from [261, 262) instead, and reports a bound on the probability of error that holds for every input (below 2−64 by default); that is not a proof, and every output says so.
  3. Candidates. The prime factors of the pivot values are the candidates. Each is settled by a run modulo that prime, in parallel; on explicit complexes, many small candidates can be settled at once by one sweep modulo their product.
  4. Numbers too hard to factor. A pivot value that resists factoring does not leave a gap. One run modulo the number N itself either splits N, or proves that all prime factors of N behave identically. The certificate then states the answer for all of them at once, for example "every prime factor of N is bad".

The output says what is guaranteed, and whether the description of the bad primes is complete. The mathematics, with proofs, is in docs/CERTIFICATE.md.

Lines starting with # are comments. There is an example of every format in examples/data/.

Understanding the output

  • prefix_k.txt holds the intervals in degree k, one per line as birth death. A death of −1 means the class never dies. This is the same format as Perseus 4, so persdia.m still plots it.
  • prefix_betti.txt holds the Betti numbers at every filtration value where they change.
  • prefix_cert.json (over Q) contains:
    • the barcode, the certificate mode and "proof" (true when everything is proved);
    • the bad primes with their differing intervals, and whether that list is complete;
    • the exact values of all non-unit pivots;
    • with --integral, --image or --torsion, the integral results (their text forms go to stderr).
    An entry marked every_prime_factor_of applies to every prime factor of the number given.
  • Without a prefix, intervals are printed to the screen in Ripser's style. Values are printed exactly: the shortest decimal that reads back as the same number.
  • Intervals of length zero are not reported (--zero-length keeps them for explicit complexes).

Beyond barcodes

Integral homology, images and torsion

For explicit complexes (simplices, cells, sset) Perseus computes exactly, over Z: the homology of the whole complex with its torsion (--integral); the image of H*(Ks; Z) → H*(Kt; Z) (--image s,t); and the torsion subgroup of Hq(Kt; Z) at every value t where it changes, with its invariant factors (--torsion). Nothing is sampled. On a random 2-complex on 40 vertices (examples/data/lm40.txt):

$ perseus simplices lm40.txt --torsion --print none
bad primes: 3 5 13  (36 non-unit pivots)
torsion of H_1(K_t; Z), where it changes:
  t = 661: Z/5
  t = 666: Z/975
  t = 667: Z/5
  t = 671: 0

Simplicial sets, nerves and classifying spaces

Finite filtered simplicial sets (normalised chains), with faces that may be degenerate: RP2 needs one simplex in each dimension. In Python, also nerves of finite categories, weighted so that they are filtered, and bar constructions BM of finite monoids and groups.

Magnitude homology

MHk,l(G; Z) of a graph, directed or not, with its torsion, for lengths up to --lmax; --pairs restricts to chosen endpoints. On the graphs where both finish, it agrees with gramag (which computes ranks over F2) and is 7–9× faster on inputs with more than 100,000 generators.

Laurent coefficients

Chain complexes over Z[t, t−1]: infinite cyclic covers, Alexander modules, Novikov homology. Perseus gives the barcode over Q(t) (which counts Novikov Betti numbers), the irreducible polynomials and the primes for which the residue fields give a different barcode, and the homology over Q[t, t−1] and over Fp[t, t−1] where it differs from the rational answer.

Finite groups

For a finite solvable group given by a polycyclic presentation (pcgroup), Hn(G; Z) exactly and the mod-p cohomology ring (dimensions, minimal generators, cup products), through Anick's resolution rather than the bar construction. Any finite group goes through its Sylow subgroups (sylow), and a chain of homomorphisms gives the induced maps on mod-p homology and their persistence (pcseries; for normal series these are Ellis and King's persistence matrices). The GAP exporters in tools/ write the inputs:

gap> Read("tools/export_sylow.g"); WritePerseusSylow(MathieuGroup(11), "m11.txt");
$ perseus sylow m11.txt --maxdim 8 --ring 2

Python

import numpy as np, perseus
r = perseus.rips(points=X, maxdim=2)               # or distances=D
r["barcode"][1]                                    # H1 as an (m, 2) array
r["certificate"]["proof"]                          # True: proved
perseus.bad_primes(r)                              # e.g. [2]
r["certificate"]["bad_primes"][2]["only_over_Fp"]  # what changes over F_2
perseus.cubical(image, periodic=[True, False])     # NaN = absent cell
perseus.flag([(0, 1, 0.5), (1, 2, 0.7)], vertex_births=[0, 0, 0.2])
perseus.simplices([((0, 1, 2), 1.0), ((1, 2, 3), 2.5)], torsion=True)
perseus.cells([(0, 0.0, []), (1, 1.0, []), (2, 2.0, [(1, 12)])], integral=True)
perseus.simplicial_set([(0, 0, []), (1, 0, [0, 0]), (2, 1, [1, (0, [0]), 1])], integral=True)   # RP^2
perseus.magnitude(edges, lmax=6)
perseus.laurent([(0, 0, []), (1, 0, [(0, "t - 1")])])
perseus.pc_group(**perseus.read_pcgroup("g.txt"), maxdim=8, ring=2)
perseus.rips(points=X, maxdim=2, coeff=2)          # just F_2

Intervals come as numpy arrays, with death inf for classes that never die. Every function takes the options of the command line (coeff, mode, fp, threads, …). The examples cover every function.

Old Perseus files

Perseus 6 does not read the Perseus 4/5 input formats (cubtop, scubtop, simtop, nmfsimtop, cellcomp, distmat, corrmat, rips, brips) directly. Instead, convert them:

python3 tools/convert_legacy.py cubtop old.txt new.txt
# prints the matching command, e.g.:  perseus cubical new.txt --coeff 2 --periodic 1

The converter reproduces the old semantics exactly, and the test suite checks converted files against the Perseus 5 executable. The printed command uses --coeff 2, because Perseus 4 and 5 computed over F2; drop it to get the rational barcode and its bad primes. Other changes from the old programs:

  • Birth times can be any real numbers; the old restriction to positive integers is gone.
  • output_betti.txt lists Betti numbers at every value where they change.
  • For Rips-type files, the old programs reported spurious infinite intervals in their top degree. The converter caps --maxdim just below it.
  • The compiler problems with Clang and recent gcc are gone.

Older versions remain available: Linux, macOS, Windows, source (Perseus 4 beta). The old example files are still here: cubgrid.txt, scubtop.txt, simtop.txt, nmfst.txt, brips.txt, rips.txt, distmat.txt.

How it works, and credits

Perseus combines many published ideas with some of its own:

  • persistent cohomology with clearing (de Silva–Morozov–Vejdemo-Johansson; Chen–Kerber);
  • Ripser's implicit coboundaries and apparent pairs (Bauer);
  • edge collapse (Boissonnat–Pritam; Glisse–Pritam) and active enumeration for H1 (Kapulkin–Kershaw's RedZeD);
  • filtered discrete Morse theory (Mischaikow–Nanda, the engine of Perseus 4 and 5, back in the cubical gradient engine), with the discrete gradients of grey-scale images (Robins–Wood–Sheppard) and union-find on critical cells (Guillou–Vidal–Tierny);
  • the dual-then-primal union-finds of GUDHI's image algorithm (Glisse), for the planar engine, and implicit cubical persistence (Kaji–Sudo–Ahara's Cubical Ripser);
  • bit-tree working columns (PHAT);
  • multi-field persistence (Boissonnat–Maria), the torsion characterisation of field dependence (Obayashi–Yoshiwaki), and interval decompositions over a PID (Luo–Henselman-Petrusek);
  • multi-modular computer algebra (rational reconstruction after Wang), integer factoring (Pollard–Brent) and Howell forms;
  • Anick resolutions as Morse complexes of rewriting systems (Anick; Brown; Sköldberg; Jöllenbeck–Welker), with G. Ellis's HAP and the Green–King database of cohomology rings as ground truth.

What is new is, above all, the certificate: an exact rational reduction and the classification of every candidate prime, including those that cannot be factored, with a theorem identifying the bad primes with the torsion of relative homology. A full catalogue, saying what is new and what is not, is in docs/TECHNIQUES.md, and the proofs behind the certificate are in docs/CERTIFICATE.md.
